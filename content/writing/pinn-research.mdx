This started as a final project for CS066 and did not stop when the semester did. The first version of this post was about learning the method: reimplementing Raissi, reading DeepXDE's source, building a Stiff-PINN curriculum for a combustion ODE, and running sweeps on a GPU that was dramatically overqualified for the job. That's still the first section. The rest is what happened when I pointed all of it at one real model for a summer, with Nate Kim and our advisor, and found out where it stops working.

## the short version of the spring

A physics-informed neural network is trained to satisfy a differential equation, not just to fit points. You move the equation to one side so a correct solution makes it zero, evaluate that on the network's output at scattered points, and add whatever's left to the loss. Autodiff gives you the derivatives of the network with respect to its inputs for free. No mesh, no discretization, and the physics does the job data usually does, which is why you can fit with very few noisy observations.

The lessons that survived: the weight on the physics term relative to the data term changes everything, adaptive weighting by gradient magnitude beat fixed weights on everything I tried, Fourier feature embeddings fix the case where a plain MLP learns the smooth part and stalls on the oscillatory part, and L-BFGS after Adam converges better than either alone. Also that the GPU is not for the network, which is tiny. It's for running thirty experiments at once.

## the model

Seven ordinary differential equations for a colorectal cancer signaling network. The states are β-catenin, APC, HOXA5, HOXA13, MYC, retinoic acid, and CYP26A1, and they form three loops: a pro-stemness loop where β-catenin drives MYC drives HOXA13 drives β-catenin, a differentiation loop where retinoic acid drives HOXA5 drives APC which degrades β-catenin, and a homeostasis loop where RA induces the enzyme that clears it. The full model our advisor had was 27 species in MATLAB. The first thing I did was port it to Python, `solve_ivp` with a stiff solver, and match the MATLAB trajectories to under 1% error, because I didn't want to be debugging a translation for the rest of the summer. The seven-state version is a reduction of that, down to the nodes the biology question actually turns on, nondimensionalized so one time unit is roughly an hour.

Two knobs define disease. W is the WNT drive coming into the system and θP is how much functional APC you have left. Normal is (0.8, 1.0). Early adenoma is (1.0, 0.75). Advanced adenoma is (1.5, 0.5). Severe APC loss is (2.0, 0.25). Everything downstream, including a stemness score built from the states, follows from those two numbers plus 34 rate constants, Hill thresholds, and time scales. That's the 36 unknowns.

The forward problem is: given all 36, simulate. The inverse problem is: given noisy time series from an experiment, recover the 36. The inverse problem is the one that matters, because the biology question is "how much APC has this tumor lost," and that's θP.

## the fit is not the answer

The first thing I learned this summer is that the picture above means much less than it looks like it means. The network reproduces the β-catenin curve to within the noise, and the parameters that produced that curve are off by 20% in θP and over 100% in a few of the rate constants. Lots of different parameter vectors produce the same trajectory. That isn't a PINN problem, it's the problem, and every method I tried this summer was a different way of asking how bad it is.

Early on we ran a two-parameter version, just W and θP with everything else known. W came back fine in every regime. θP came back within 1% in the normal regime and 142% off in severe APC loss. The reason is in the equation: APC degradation is `1 + δ(1 − θP)`, so at θP = 1 the δ term vanishes and θP and δ can't be told apart at all, and at low θP the system saturates, so large changes in θP barely move the curves. The parameter the model is most sensitive to overall turns out to be the least identifiable where it matters. That θP wall shows up in every method below.

The full 36-parameter inverse problem, with an autodiff residual, got 6 of 36 within 10% in the normal regime. That's the baseline.

## the derivative problem

I spent a while assuming that was a data problem. It wasn't. Denser, cleaner observations made W worse, not better, going from 0.80 down to 0.50. Gradient matching on frozen networks gave 0.63. Something in the loss itself was biased.

The residual in a standard PINN uses `dz/dt` computed by autodiff through the network. The network is a smooth function that passes close to the data, but its derivative is nobody's target and it's noisy in a way that doesn't average out. When you ask the optimizer to make `dz/dt − f(z, θ)` zero and `dz/dt` is wrong by a small systematic amount, the cheapest fix is to bend θ, not the network. You get a beautiful trajectory and a biased parameter.

The fix is to not differentiate. Between adjacent collocation points the residual becomes the trapezoid rule: `[z(t₂) − z(t₁)] − ½Δt[f(z₂) + f(z₁)]`. It only uses values the network outputs, not their derivatives, and it's the same integral form as the ODE solver that generated the reference data in the first place. Same networks, same optimizer, same data, and the integral residual recovers twice as many of the 36 parameters as the autodiff version. Counted as parameters within 10% across the four regimes, 26 became 50. That's the one methodological result I'd defend from the summer. A classical least-squares ODE fit with a stiff solver is still a strong baseline on the same data, and the PINN's real advantage isn't a point estimate. It's that the same machinery extends to the Bayesian and hybrid versions below, which the classical fit doesn't.

The other thing that helped was experimental design, and it helped less than I expected. We started with six conditions that differ only in the retinoic acid protocol: control, no ATRA, early ATRA, late ATRA, low dose, strong circadian. Every one of them enters the WNT/HOX/MYC sub-network through a single door, the RA to HOXA5 term, so the parameters behind that door don't move. Adding WNT pulse, WNT inhibition, MYC pulse and a combo condition helped the classical fit a lot and the PINN a little.

## how many are identifiable, four ways

**Fisher information.** Finite-difference sensitivities of every state to every parameter at 200 time points across all ten conditions, 14,000 observations, `FIM = SᵀS/σ²`. The condition numbers are 10⁷ to 10¹⁹. Each regime has exactly one null direction, and it's the θP/δ direction: pure δ at normal, mixing into θP as WNT drive rises. Nine parameters are cleanly identifiable in every regime, all of them rate constants on the RA and HOX side. The first run of this script said all 36 were identifiable because I read the diagonal of a pseudoinverse; I rewrote it the same afternoon to look at null-space participation instead.

<Figure src="/writing/pinn-research/fim-spectra.png" alt="Fisher information eigenvalue spectra for four regimes, log scale, each collapsing by many orders of magnitude at the last eigenvalue" caption="one eigenvalue per regime falls off a cliff. that's the θP/δ direction. the gentle slope before it is what sloppiness looks like." />

**Profile likelihoods.** Fix one parameter at a grid of values, refit the other 35, see whether the likelihood has a real minimum. Slow and the most honest of the four. Sixteen identifiable, six marginal, fourteen not.

**Sobol sensitivity.** Global variance decomposition with SALib: sample the parameter space, simulate, and ask what fraction of the variance in each state each parameter explains, alone and with interactions. θP dominates. On β-catenin its first-order and total-order indices are both about 0.8, meaning the model's output depends on it more than on anything else and barely through interactions. Which is exactly the parameter the Fisher matrix says you can't pin down at low APC. Sensitivity and identifiability are different questions, and this model is the cleanest example I've seen of a parameter scoring high on one and low on the other, because the response to θP saturates.

Those three agree with each other, which is the point of running three. The fourth is the one that puts error bars on it.

**Bayesian PINN.** Freeze the networks from the integral-residual run, treat the 36 parameters as unknowns with log-normal priors, and run Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the trapezoidal residual as the likelihood. The first run reported 36 of 36 identifiable with an effective sample size of three. I'd used the raw residual count, 279,930 terms, as if they were independent observations, which makes the likelihood absurdly sharp and the chain stuck. The right effective count is the number of actual observations, 2,800, scaled so reduced chi-squared is one at the MAP. After that fix the chains mix on some parameters and not others, and no run passes the gate I set for it (median ESS above 200 and credible intervals covering at least 33 of 36 truths), even at 3,000 draws and 48 leapfrog steps, which is three and a half hours per regime.

W posteriors are tight and near truth except in severe APC loss; θP posteriors either flatten to the prior or spike at 1.0 far from the true value" caption="W is recoverable. θP isn't. in early adenoma the posterior is flat across the whole prior; in severe APC loss it's a spike at the wrong end." />

The picture is worth more than the gate. W is tight in three regimes and biased low in the fourth. θP either reverts to the prior or converges confidently to 1.0 when the truth is 0.25, a z-score in the thousands. That's the same wall as the two-parameter experiment in June, now with error bars.

The forward Bayesian PINN, parameters known and the network weights sampled, worked. Predictive coverage 0.94 to 0.95 in every regime, with the caveat that you have to report the predictive band, not the reconstruction band. The reconstruction band alone under-covers at 0.56 to 0.73 because it only carries epistemic uncertainty and ignores the observation noise. I made that mistake first and the coverage number looked like a bug for a day.

## hybrids

The last thing we tried is replacing one Hill term with a small network and asking whether it learns the right shape. Universal differential equations, basically. The network is two layers of five tanh units, 46 weights, wrapped as `softplus(g(x)) · (1 − e^(−x/x₀))` so it's non-negative and zero at zero, trained jointly with the remaining 34 mechanistic parameters.

Replacing the WNT-to-MYC term, `η b²/(κ² + b²)`, gives a learned curve within 6 to 10% of the true Hill function across the observed range. It also costs you the rest of the MYC equation. The basal MYC production constant comes back 30 to 50% wrong in every regime because the learned function absorbs it: the network can produce a constant offset at low β-catenin as easily as it can produce a curve, and nothing in the data distinguishes the two. Replacing the RA-to-HOXA5 term learns the right shape 10 to 25% low, and the zero-at-zero anchor never binds because the observed range doesn't get close to zero. The unconstrained version fits better.

<Figure src="/writing/pinn-research/hybrid-wnt-myc.png" alt="Four panels comparing the learned neural term for WNT to MYC against the true Hill function across four regimes, NRMSE 6 to 10%, with the learned curve offset upward at low β-catenin" caption="the learned WNT → MYC term against the truth. the shape is right. the offset at the left edge is the basal production constant being absorbed." />

The one hybrid that worked cleanly is the APC one, and it worked because of how it was trained. The unknown is the degradation multiplier as a function of APC loss, `f(1 − θP)`. Train on four regimes with θP at 1, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, hold out three intermediate values, and the learned function reproduces the held-out truth to 0.12% with `f(0) = 0` exactly and monotone throughout. Then freeze it and recover θP for a new regime. Two stages, calibrate then infer, and the function is learned across regimes instead of within one.

I'd summarize the hybrid results as: a network can learn a mechanism when you can vary the input to that mechanism across experiments, and when you can't, it learns whatever is cheapest, which is usually a constant that belongs to some other term.

## compute

A 64-core box with one GPU, float64 throughout. Four regimes in parallel take 56 of the cores. There's no scheduler; the queue is a shell script in tmux that watches for a DONE line and launches the next variant. A hybrid variant, three restarts across four regimes, is about an hour and forty-five minutes. Inverse HMC is three and a half hours per regime. One edge atlas run died after 2.8 GPU-hours to a bug that would have been caught by a two-minute dry run, which I now do.

Things I got wrong along the way, for the record: the pseudoinverse Fisher matrix, the ESS-of-three posterior, a cosine learning-rate schedule that froze the physical parameters before the networks had converged, and a best-of-three restart rule that picks by physics loss and in two regimes threw away the start with better parameter recovery. A third-stage refinement step with frozen networks is in every run and has never changed a result.

---

The old ending of this post said PINNs are narrower than the enthusiasm suggests and good inside that range: known physics, not much data, a need to explain the answer. I'd keep that and add one thing. On an inverse problem, the method you use to fit the parameters matters much less than whether the parameters are identifiable at all, and PINNs make it very easy to not notice, because the trajectory always looks right. The most useful things I built this summer were the ones that tell you when not to trust the fit.
